Note

Sources for this page: the course’s own introduction slides from earlier years, updated for this version of the course. Dates, rooms, deadlines and this year’s teaching assistants are on Canvas, not here. For an overview of the course, how a session works, and where to find everything, start with Session 0.

This page holds the rules of the course: what you need to do to pass, how the grade is set, and who to contact.

Exam and requirements

The course has three mandatory parts:

  • The labs are pass/fail, and must all be passed to pass the course (see Labs below). Doing them on time gives bonus points on the exam (see Bonus points below).
  • The project is graded 0-100, and you need at least 40% to pass it. It has three graded parts: the code you have written, the final report, and an oral presentation of your code to one of the teaching assistants. See the Capstone page for the task and the grading criteria.
  • The exam is a pen-and-paper exam, graded 0-100; you need at least 40% to pass it. You may bring up to five pages (single-sided) of your own notes.

The final grade is 70% from the exam and 30% from the project, and you need 50% in total to pass. The grade scale (not exactly the default one at SU) is:

Grade A B C D E Fx F
Total score ≥ 90% ≥ 80% ≥ 70% ≥ 60% ≥ 50% ≥ 45% < 45%

Bonus points on the exam

Up to 9 points are added to your score on the main exam (0-100), 3 for each of three things. For each, doing all of it gives 3 points, and every one you miss or do late takes away one point.

  • Questions before the lecture: up to 3 points. Before each lecture from Session 2 on, send in on Canvas what you want explained: a question, or a topic you want more time on. One line is enough. The Canvas survey “Questions before the lecture” closes at 18:00 the day before the lecture, so that the teacher can read your questions and answer the most common ones at the start of the lecture.
  • Being at the lectures: up to 3 points. Every lecture has a short live quiz on quiz.bioinfo.se, which you answer on your phone after logging in with your university account. Answering it counts as being present (at least three quarters of its questions); whether your answers are right or wrong is never used in grading. Sessions 0 and 1 are one lecture, so there are 12 lectures. Each of the two Q&A sessions you attend (by answering its check-in question when you arrive, logged in) makes up for one missed lecture.
  • Labs on time: up to 3 points. A lab is on time when you have passed its lab quiz and filled in its feedback survey by the due date (see Labs). A late lab still has to be passed to pass the course.

The bonus points count only on the main exam, not on a re-exam, and only once. If you fail the exam, a re-exam is normally offered a few weeks after the course; if you have already passed (grade E or higher), there is no retake to improve the grade. Past exams are on the Schedule page. Two question-and-answer sessions, one after Session 6 and one after Session 12, prepare you for the exam.

The Q&A sessions

The Q&A sessions are not lectures. Rereading and listening to the same explanation again feel useful but do little for an exam; trying to recall what you know, and finding out what you cannot do yet, does much more. So each Q&A is a practice exam with immediate feedback:

  • Recall (7 min): with books closed, write down everything you remember about two or three central topics.
  • Practice exam (37 min): four exam-style questions, on paper, with the same aids as in the exam: bring your pages of notes.
  • Break (15 min).
  • Marking in pairs (20 min): you mark each other’s answers with the marking guide, so you see what a full answer looks like. The teaching assistants help.
  • What the room got wrong (30 min): an anonymous poll shows which questions were hardest, and the teacher goes through those and your questions.
  • What to study now (8 min): you write down your two weakest topics.

Nothing is handed in or graded, and the poll is anonymous. The only thing recorded is a logged-in check-in question when you arrive: attending a Q&A makes up for one missed lecture (see Bonus points). You can post questions anonymously during the session, or ask them on the Canvas forum before it.

Labs

Most sessions have a lab (see the Course Schedule): a notebook in this book, with a short online quiz on Canvas.

  • Mandatory. Every lab quiz must be passed, with at least 50% correct.
  • Due before the next lecture. Each lab quiz is due at 23:59 the evening before the next lecture (the last one the evening before the exam; when the next lecture is the morning after the lab, you get one more day), so that you come to each lecture with the previous lab done; the dates are on Canvas.
  • Available as soon as published. You do not have to wait for the lecture.
  • Feedback after each lab. The last part of every lab is a short feedback survey on Canvas, due with the lab quiz. It is anonymous: Canvas records that you filled it in, but nobody can see what you wrote. It asks for concrete suggestions on the lab and the session’s material (what was too long, what background was missing, errors, unclear instructions), so that we can improve the course. Sessions 0 and 1, which have no lab, have the same kind of survey, filled in at the end of their lecture. Errors and suggestions can also be sent at any time with the feedback form.
  • One redo chance if you fail, normally only the next time the course runs, and the lab may have changed by then.

Expected workload

The course is full time for four weeks and two days: about 176 hours of work, at eight hours a day. The table below gives our estimate of where that time goes: each session has its lecture (three hours; Sessions 0 and 1 share one), reading the material and doing the lab, and then come the capstone project and the exam.

  • Lab is the total time for the lab, including its quiz on Canvas. Every lab is designed to fit into the scheduled three-hour lab afternoon, and most students need about two to two and a half hours. Optional extras in the lab notebooks are not included.

Plagiarism

Plagiarism has unfortunately been an issue in earlier years. You may already have signed a plagiarism policy form; if not, you will be given one. Please set an example of honesty for your peers, since not everyone has fully understood the rules. If you see a problem, or have a suggestion for how to handle it, talk to the teachers. In general we trust everyone: you are honest until proven otherwise.

Plagiarism is copying the work of others, whether from online or printed sources, your peers, or anyone else, without attributing it directly and explicitly. Discussing methods and approaches with other students is encouraged; submitting someone else’s answers, code or text as your own is not.

  • All written reports are checked for plagiarism.
  • Plagiarism can lead to suspension from the university.

If you are unsure where the line is on a specific assignment, ask before you submit, not after.

AI tools

You may use AI tools during the course, but not during the exam. The final report and the labs must not be written by AI tools, but you may use them as a “science buddy”, for example to understand a method or to improve your report. Session 2’s “How to use chatbots” section shows how to use them well.
