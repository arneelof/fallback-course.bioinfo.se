Lab notebook: download session06-lab.ipynb (the same link the students get) and open it in VS Code.

A hands-on PyMOL tutorial on three real structures (an HIV Env/antibody complex, a chitinase with real bound metals, and human neuroglobin), plus a TM-align comparison, a Foldseek search of the whole PDB and a structural-domain lookup. Everything runs in the lab notebook, notebooks/session06-lab.ipynb, in VS Code on a Colab machine: the notebook installs PyMOL as a Python library, so you type PyMOL commands inside cmd.do(“…”) and see the result with snapshot(). Nothing needs to be installed on your own computer. See the book page’s “Looking at structures: PyMOL” section for the representations (cartoon/sticks/spheres) this lab uses.

Section A -- loading a real structure and looking at it

Run parts 1 and 2 of the lab notebook. They fetch a real cryo-EM structure, 7LXM – a real HIV envelope glycoprotein trimer in complex with a real broadly neutralizing antibody, PGT122 (cmd.do("fetch 7LXM, type=pdb, async=0"); the type=pdb matters here – PyMOL’s default mmCIF fetch assigns extra chain letters to individual sugars/waters, which would give a much larger, misleading chain count than the real polymer-chain count below).

The notebook colours the structure by secondary structure (color grey60, 7LXM then color red, 7LXM and ss H, color yellow, 7LXM and ss S, color blue, 7LXM and ss L+'') in PyMOL’s cartoon representation, then by chain (util.cbc) instead. Look at both pictures, and read the COMPND lines PyMOL prints when it fetches the entry.

Section B -- extracting chains into separate objects

Part 3 of the notebook colours the whole structure grey again (color grey60, 7LXM), then extracts chains H, N, and P into three separate PyMOL objects (create obj_H, 7LXM and chain H, and similarly for N and P) – these are the three copies of one component, one per protomer of the trimer.

Section C -- structural alignment: do two chains share a fold?

cealign structurally aligns two objects and reports a real RMSD – the same measure the Session 6 book page’s “Comparing proteins in 3D” part introduces. The notebook aligns two of the three chains from Section B against each other and draws them on top of each other:

result = cmd.cealign("obj_H", "obj_N")
print(result["RMSD"], result["alignment_length"])

Optional tip (not part of the quiz) -- viewing a structure directly in VS Code

PyMOL is this course’s main structure tool, but sometimes you just want a quick look without writing any code. Install the real Protein Viewer extension (ArianJamasb.protein-viewer on the VS Code Marketplace, built on Mol* – the same viewer RCSB and PDBe use on their own websites): open VS Code’s Extensions panel (Ctrl/Cmd+Shift+X), search “Protein Viewer”, install it. Then open the Command Palette (Ctrl/Cmd+Shift+P), run “Start Protein Viewer”, and enter a real PDB accession – try 8C6Z from Section D.

Section E -- TM-align and a Foldseek search

Run parts 5 and 6 of the lab notebook. Part 5 uses TM-align (through the tmtools package) to compare 1OJ6 (human neuroglobin, real X-ray structure, chain A) with three real structures: its own real AlphaFold DB prediction (same protein, two independent methods), 2HHB (a related but different real globin, this course’s own running example), and 1EMA (a real, structurally unrelated fold, GFP). It is the three-tier comparison of the book page’s “Comparing proteins in 3D” part: same protein (experimental vs. predicted), same fold (a different globin), unrelated fold. Today AlphaFold DB simply supplies a structure to compare; Session 12 asks how that prediction is made and which parts of it to trust. Part 6 then sends neuroglobin to the Foldseek web server (through its API) and searches every structure in the PDB.

Section F -- real structural domain classification
